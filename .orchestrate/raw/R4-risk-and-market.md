## 1. Case law timeline

| Case | Year(s) | Holding | What it actually means for us |
|---|---|---|---|
| **hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn** — CFAA phase | 2017 (district PI) → 2019 (9th Cir. "hiQ I") → 2021 (SCOTUS GVR after *Van Buren*) → **April 18, 2022** (9th Cir. "hiQ II", 31 F.4th 1180) | 9th Circuit again affirmed a preliminary injunction *forcing LinkedIn to let hiQ keep scraping* purely public, logged-out profile pages, holding hiQ raised a "serious question" that CFAA "without authorization" doesn't cleanly apply where access isn't gated behind a login at all — the "gates-up-or-down" framework from *Van Buren* (2021). [Justia opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/17-16783/17-16783-2022-04-18.html), [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HiQ_Labs_v._LinkedIn), [White & Case analysis](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking/web-scraping-website-terms-and-cfaa-hiqs-preliminary-injunction-affirmed-again). | **This is the claim everyone misreads as "scraping is legal."** It is not a final merits ruling — it's a preliminary-injunction posture, decided only on "likelihood of success," and it applies *narrowly* to unauthenticated access to data LinkedIn had made technically public with no login wall. It says nothing about breach of contract, and nothing about logged-in/authenticated automation — which is exactly what linkedin-relay would be. |

[Proskauer write-up](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2022/11/11/court-finds-hiq-breached-linkedins-terms-prohibiting-scraping-but-in-mixed-ruling-declines-to-grant-summary-judgment-to-either-party-as-to-certain-key-issues/), [settlement](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2022/12/08/hiq-and-linkedin-reach-proposed-settlement-in-landmark-scraping-case/). | **This is the part that actually bites.** LinkedIn's real weapon was never the CFAA — it's the User Agreement as an enforceable contract, plus state trespass-to-chattels/computer-access law, especially once fake accounts or authenticated access were involved. hiQ ultimately lost, on the merits, on almost every non-CFAA theory. Net: "hiQ won" is popular myth; hiQ won a preliminary skirmish on one narrow legal theory (CFAA for public data) and then lost the war and paid $500k. |
| **LinkedIn v. Consent judgment: permanently delete all scraped data, destroy the scraping software, stop accessing LinkedIn by automation. No damages paid, no admission of liability. [LinkedIn's own announcement](https://news.linkedin.com/2022/may/an-update-on-scraping), [Law360](https://www.law360.com/articles/1491564/linkedin-settles-lawsuit-against-mantheos-over-data-scrape). | Confirms LinkedIn treats **authenticated bulk scraping via fake/paid accounts** as squarely actionable — this is far closer to a "company selling scraped LinkedIn data" pattern than a personal tool, but it's the template LinkedIn's legal team reaches for. |
| **LinkedIn/Microsoft v. Nubela (Proxycurl)** | Filed **Jan 24, 2025**, N.D. Cal. → settled/wound down **mid-2025**, Proxycurl announced shutdown **July 2025** | Six claims: breach of contract, fraud/deceit, **CFAA**, California UCL, Lanham Act, misappropriation. Alleged "hundreds of thousands" of fake accounts scraping millions of profiles, run as a $10M ARR commercial API business (bootstrapped, no VC). Settlement required permanent deletion of all LinkedIn-derived data and a **permanent injunction** against future unauthorized access. Founder said explicitly they couldn't afford to fight a multi-billion-dollar litigant. [Proxycurl's own goodbye post](https://nubela.co/blog/goodbye-proxycurl/), [coverage](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-wins-legal-case-data-scrapers-proxycurl/756101/). | The most commercially serious LinkedIn-data reseller to date, killed not by a lost merits ruling but by **litigation cost asymmetry** — LinkedIn/Microsoft can outspend almost anyone. This is the single most important commercial-viability data point for section 5. |
| **LinkedIn v. ProAPIs** + **~100 unnamed "John Doe" individual scrapers** | 2024–2025 (reported via [SecurityAffairs](https://securityaffairs.com/183001/security/linkedin-sues-proapis-for-15k-month-linkedin-data-scraping-scheme.html), [The Record](https://therecord.media/linkedin-sues-data-scraping-company)) | ProAPIs allegedly ran an "industrial-scale fake account mill," reselling scraped profile/company/academic data and reactions/comments (some behind the password wall) for up to **$15,000/month per customer**. LinkedIn separately sued **100 unnamed individuals** using bots to harvest profiles — a discovery vehicle (Doe defendants) specifically to unmask IP addresses/identities before naming real defendants. | This is the closest thing to "LinkedIn suing individuals" in the record, but the individuals are Doe placeholders in what is fundamentally an anti-reseller enforcement action, not evidence LinkedIn routinely sues a lone person running a personal tool for their own research. No verified case found (through this search) of LinkedIn suing a private individual for purely personal, non-resold, non-fake-account scraping of their own feed/connections. **Unverified**: whether such a case exists but wasn't newsworthy enough to surface. |

**What theories does LinkedIn actually win on?** In descending order of how much they've actually litigated to a real result: **(1) breach of contract** (User Agreement §8.2 — always pleaded, always the theory that survived on the merits in hiQ), **(2) CFAA** — but only bites cleanly once you add fake accounts / credential fraud / password-walled access, not bare public scraping, **(3) state computer-access statutes** (Cal. Penal Code §502), **(4) trespass to chattels** (server load / resource consumption theory), **(5) fraud/misrepresentation** (fake accounts, fake payment instruments), **(6) Lanham Act / UCL** (used against Proxycurl, more of an unfair-competition/trademark angle for a branded competing product). **DMCA has not shown up as LinkedIn's lead theory** in any case found here — it's a copyright takedown tool, not central to their scraping enforcement strategy. Every single litigated case above ended in a **consent judgment/settlement**, not a merits trial verdict — so there is no binding appellate precedent saying "personal-use scraping via one's own login is illegal," but there's also none saying it's protected. It has simply never been tested because everyone LinkedIn has sued settled or folded.

## 2. Where the personal-use line sits

Three distinguishable postures, ranked by what has actually drawn enforcement:

- **(c) Resale of scraped data** — this is what every litigated case above targeted (Mantheos, Proxycurl, ProAPIs). Commercial resale, at any scale, is the pattern LinkedIn's legal team is built to find and kill, and it wins nearly every time on breach-of-contract + CFAA-via-fake-accounts.
- **(b) Bulk collection of public profiles, no resale** — this is hiQ's fact pattern. Even here, hiQ *lost* once LinkedIn pursued contract/tort theories instead of just CFAA; the "public scraping is legal" holding was a narrow, preliminary, CFAA-only ruling that did not survive as a full defense.
- **(a) An individual using their own authenticated session to read/organize data they already have legitimate access to** — **no verified litigated case found** where LinkedIn sued a single individual for this pattern in isolation (no fake accounts, no resale, no bulk harvesting of others' data at scale). The Doe-defendant suits are a discovery step inside what are still reseller/bot-farm cases, not a track record of personal-tool enforcement.

That said, LinkedIn's contract explicitly does not carve out a personal-use exception for automation — see §3 below. The absence of a lawsuit against an individual is not the same as the conduct being permitted; it may just mean **enforcement so far has been ROI-driven against operations big enough to matter to LinkedIn's legal budget**, not a legal safe harbor. This distinction — "hasn't been sued" vs. "is allowed" — needs to be explicit in the design doc, not blurred.

### Verbatim ToS clauses (fetched 2026-08-01, `linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement`)

> **Section 8.2, item 2 ("Don'ts"):** "Develop, support or use software, devices, scripts, robots or any other means or processes (such as crawlers, browser plugins and add-ons or any other technology) to scrape or copy the Services or otherwise copy profiles and other data from the Services."

> **Section 8.2, item 13:** "Use bots or other unauthorized automated methods to access the Services, add or download contacts, send or redirect messages, create, comment on, like, share, or re-share posts."

> **Section 8.2, item 4:** "Copy, use, display or distribute any information (including content) obtained from the Services, whether directly or through third parties such as search tools or data aggregators or brokers, without the consent of the content owner."

> **Section 3.1 (Your License to LinkedIn):** "You promise to only provide content and other information that you have the right to share and that your LinkedIn profile will be truthful."

> **Section 3.4 (Limits):** "LinkedIn reserves the right to restrict, suspend, or terminate your account if you breach this Contract or the law or are misusing the Services."

> **Section 3.2 (Service Availability):** "LinkedIn is not a storage service. You agree that we have no obligation to store, maintain or provide you a copy of any content or other information."

**There is no clause carving out "your own automation on your own account is fine."** §8.2 is written as a flat, unconditional ban on bots/scripts/crawlers regardless of whose account is doing it or what it's used for. A personal research tool is, on the plain text, squarely inside the prohibition — the ToS makes no distinction linkedin-relay's design will be able to lean on contractually. (Caveat: I could not verify the section numbering against a live, current fetch beyond the summarized pass above — recommend a human double-check the current `linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement` page before quoting these numbers in a public design doc, since LinkedIn periodically renumbers this document.)

### Professional Community Policies (fetched 2026-08-01, `linkedin.com/legal/professional-community-policies`)

No explicit "no scraping" language here (that lives in the User Agreement) — this document is about behavioral norms: no fake profiles, no using someone else's account, no spam/bulk messaging, no artificial engagement (coordinated liking/resharing). Relevant to a design that includes confirmed write actions (comment/react/connect): **artificial engagement and "agree ahead of time to like/reshare" are explicitly named as violations**, which bears directly on any auto-react or auto-comment feature.

### robots.txt (fetched 2026-08-01, `linkedin.com/robots.txt`)

Opens with an explicit notice: **"the use of robots or other automated means to access LinkedIn without express permission is strictly prohibited,"** directs permission requests to `whitelist-crawl@linkedin.com`. Disallows crawling of profiles, connections, messaging, job search/apply, and most authenticated surfaces for all but LinkedIn's own bot and a couple of whitelisted SEO crawlers (rogerbot, deepcrawl) on narrow paths. This is consistent with, not looser than, the User Agreement — robots.txt is not a "logged-out scraping is fine" signal here since it bans automated access outright as a policy statement, not just a machine-readable directive.

## 3. The account-risk reality

Pattern reported consistently across practitioner/vendor sources (PhantomBuster, Datablist, ConnectSafely — all somewhat self-interested as automation-tool vendors, so treat specifics as practitioner-lore, not verified LinkedIn-sourced numbers):

- **Detection signals**: device fingerprinting, browser/session anomalies, IP reputation, request cadence/burst patterns, and — critically — a banned account's new replacement account is often flagged near-immediately via device/fingerprint correlation.
- **Escalation ladder**: warning/CAPTCHA challenge → **temporary restriction** (days, e.g. reported 3–14 days; can't send connection requests/messages but can still browse) → **manual review** (reported 5–10 business days) → **permanent restriction**. This ladder is consistently reported but **unverified against any LinkedIn-published SLA** — LinkedIn does not publish these numbers; they're aggregated practitioner observation.
- **Appeal**: identity verification, an in-app appeal flow, or a dedicated appeal form. Reported success rate for permanent-ban appeals is **under 15%** per one vendor source — **this figure is unverified/unsourced to LinkedIn** and should be treated as a directional practitioner claim, not a cited statistic, in the design doc.
- **What is actually lost**: unlike an X ban (an anonymous or pseudonymous identity, generally replaceable), a LinkedIn permanent ban destroys **a professional identity accumulated over years** — connections graph, endorsements/recommendations, posting history, group memberships, and often the primary channel recruiters/clients use to find the person. There is no clean "just make a new account" path since a new account tied to the same device/identity gets flagged, and even a clean new account starts with zero history. **This asymmetry — a rate-limit mistake on X costs you a burner, the same mistake on LinkedIn can cost your professional reputation graph — is the single most important framing fact for the design panel.**

## 4. GDPR / data-protection angle (relevant since the user is in Hungary/EU)

- **Article 2(2)(c)** exempts processing by a natural person for "purely personal or household activity." **GDPRhub/DPC guidance is explicit that this is interpreted narrowly**: it covers management of one's own personal/family/household affairs, and critically — **the data must not be used in connection with a professional or commercial activity, and must not be made publicly available.** [GDPRhub Art. 2](https://gdprhub.eu/Article_2_GDPR), [Irish DPC FAQ](https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/faqs/general/what-household-exemption).
- **This exemption is fragile for linkedin-relay's actual use case.** If the tool is used for job-search research, sales prospecting, recruiting, or any activity connected to the user's professional life (which is the entire point of LinkedIn), the "purely personal" characterization gets shaky — courts/DPAs weigh purpose, not just who's doing the processing. A local cache built explicitly to support career/business research is arguably **not** purely personal/household, even though only one person uses it and nothing is shared.
- Separately, **GDPR governs the underlying scraping act regardless of the household exemption**, per the EDPB's new **Guidelines 03/2026 on web scraping** (adopted July 8, 2026 — very fresh, in the middle of the AI-training-data controversy but the reasoning applies generally): **public visibility of data is not a legal basis and is not consent.** [EDPB PDF](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-07/edpb_guidelines_2020603_webscraping_v1_en_0.pdf). Where a legal basis is needed (i.e., wherever the household exemption doesn't clearly apply), **legitimate interest (Art. 6(1)(f)) is the realistic basis**, requiring a documented three-part Legitimate Interest Assessment: (1) purpose test — genuine, articulated interest (personal career research plausibly qualifies; mass profiling doesn't); (2) necessity test — least-invasive method achieving the purpose; (3) balancing test — proportionate scope, retention no longer than needed, de-identification where feasible, and — per the EDPB — **explicit weight given to whether the source site deploys technical/contractual anti-scraping measures** (robots.txt, login walls, CAPTCHAs) as a signal that data subjects had no reasonable expectation of being scraped. **LinkedIn fails this test in the source's favor** — its robots.txt and ToS both explicitly prohibit automated access, which an EDPB-style analysis would treat as evidence against a "no reasonable expectation of privacy" argument.
- **Concrete implications for the local cache** (`~/.lnrelay`): (1) Storing *other people's* profile/post data (not just the user's own) is processing third-party personal data and likely falls outside the household exemption once purpose is professional; (2) minimize to what's needed for the stated research purpose — don't cache full profiles of everyone encountered, prefer caching only what the user explicitly saved/bookmarked or authored; (3) **must support deletion on request and a defined retention ceiling** — a GDPR-minded design should default to time-boxed retention (e.g., auto-purge cached third-party data after N days unless the user explicitly pins it) and a hard `--purge`/`--delete-all` command; (4) since this is the user's own local machine and own account, there's no third party "controller" liability exposure to worry about in the DPA-enforcement sense, but the legal analysis above should still shape defaults, since it's the right thing to build and reduces exposure if the cache is ever inspected, subpoenaed, or the account is investigated.

**Answer to the panel's actual question**: yes, a legitimate paid API exists (LinkedIn's own Partner Programs), but it is **structurally unavailable** to this project — it requires being an incorporated company, months of approval, and enterprise pricing aimed at recruiting/marketing/sales verticals, not a personal research assistant. The closest *commercial* analog to what the user wants (Unipile) is itself an unofficial, session-riding integration — it has just survived longer than the resale-focused competitors, plausibly because it doesn't build a third-party data lake LinkedIn can order deleted, and doesn't resell data at scale.

## 6. The compliant-design toolkit (what survivors actually do differently)

Synthesizing the pattern across every case above (what got sued vs. what hasn't):

1. **Own-session only, never a shared data lake.** Every losing party (Mantheos, Proxycurl, ProAPIs) built a multi-tenant cache of *other people's* LinkedIn data, servable/resellable to third parties. That data lake is precisely what a court can order permanently deleted (as happened twice) — and it's the thing that makes the operation big enough to be worth suing. A single local `~/.lnrelay` cache, scoped to one person's own account, one machine, never resold, is categorically different in both litigation-target economics and GDPR posture.
2. **No fake accounts, no credential fraud.** Every CFAA claim that actually stuck (hiQ's final judgment, Mantheos, Proxycurl, ProAPIs) involved fake/purchased accounts or fraudulently obtained subscriptions to reach password-walled data. A tool using the *user's own legitimate, paid-for-if-applicable* account and cookies avoids this specific, heavily-litigated theory entirely.
3. **Human-in-the-loop on all writes.** Confirmed-only actions (matching x-relay's existing `--confirm` pattern) keep the tool from ever executing bulk connect/message/react sprees — which is both the GDPR-legitimate-interest "necessity" argument and the anti-"artificial engagement" Professional Community Policies argument.
4. **Rate limits below human-plausible thresholds, no headless-farm patterns.** Jittered timing, request volumes that look like a person clicking around, not a script — the same posture x-relay already documents for X ("assumes residential IP... any account can be rate-limited or banned").
5. **Minimize and time-box the local cache.** Cache what the user already has legitimate access to and actually wants to keep (their own posts, saved items, direct connections), not an ever-growing scrape of every profile encountered; default retention window with an explicit purge command, both because it reduces GDPR exposure and because it reduces "what does this look like if the account is ever investigated."
6. **Respect robots.txt/ToS as a documented risk acknowledgment, not a false claim of compliance.** linkedin-relay's docs should say plainly, the way x-relay's CLAUDE.md already does for X, that this **violates LinkedIn's User Agreement §8.2** and carries real account-ban risk — never claim the tool is "ToS-compliant" the way vendor marketing pages do, since on the plain text of §8.2 it structurally is not.
7. **No multi-account, no farm, no resale — ever**, matching the project's own stated non-goals. This is the load-bearing difference between "one person automating their own reading" and every pattern that has actually drawn a lawsuit.

## Hard constraints (design requirements, not opinions)

- **MUST** operate on exactly one user-owned, user-authenticated LinkedIn session (own `li_at`/CSRF cookies) — never proxy, aggregate, or serve another person's session.
- **MUST NOT** persist or export a multi-subject dataset intended for resale, sharing, or third-party consumption. The local cache is single-user, single-machine, non-transferable by design.
- **MUST NOT** use fake/secondary/purchased accounts under any circumstance — this is the single highest-litigation-risk pattern in the entire case record.
- **MUST** gate every write action (connect, message, react, comment, follow) behind explicit human confirmation; no autonomous bulk write loops.
- **MUST** rate-limit and jitter all requests to stay under LinkedIn's own detection thresholds as best-understood, and fail loudly rather than silently retry-hammering on a 429/challenge response.
- **MUST** cap local retention of third-party (non-owner) profile/post data to a defined, short default window, with a working delete/purge command, to keep the GDPR legitimate-interest balancing test and the household-exemption argument as strong as they can be.
- **MUST NOT** claim ToS compliance in documentation; **MUST** state plainly that this breaches LinkedIn's User Agreement §8.2 and carries real, non-zero risk of temporary or permanent account restriction, with no guarantee of a successful appeal.
- **MUST NOT** pursue mass harvesting of public profiles at hiQ/Proxycurl scale, even without resale — the volume itself, not just the resale, is what has drawn suits.
- **SHOULD** document, next to the risk section, that no official/paid API route exists for an individual (Partner Programs require incorporation), so the reader understands this isn't a corner-cutting choice but a structural gap in LinkedIn's own offering.
